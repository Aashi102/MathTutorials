Foundations

Foundations are about getting comfortable with the basic objects of algebra: numbers, variables, and operations. You’ll also learn the order of operations so expressions always have a clear meaning.

Key idea: A variable is just a placeholder for a number.

Example: In x + 5, x could be 2, 10, or 1000.

  • Identify variables and constants in expressions.
  • Apply order of operations to mixed expressions.
  • Recognize when an expression is ambiguous or unclear.

Expressions

Expressions are how we represent quantities that might change. You’ll practice simplifying them, combining like terms, and evaluating them for specific values.

Key idea: Like terms have the same variable part.

Example: 3x and 5x are like terms; 3x and 3x² are not.

  • Simplify expressions by combining like terms.
  • Evaluate expressions for given values of x.
  • Translate verbal descriptions into algebraic expressions.
Open Expressions Lesson

Equations

Equations are statements that two expressions are equal. Solving an equation means finding all values of the variable that make the statement true.

Key idea: Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other.

Example: To solve x + 7 = 15, subtract 7 from both sides.

  • Solve one‑step equations using inverse operations.
  • Solve two‑step equations by undoing operations in order.
  • Check solutions by substituting back into the original equation.
Open Equations Lesson

Inequalities

Inequalities compare quantities instead of setting them equal. Their solutions are often ranges of values, not just a single number.

Key idea: Multiplying or dividing by a negative flips the inequality sign.

Example: −2x ≤ 10 becomes x ≥ −5.

  • Graph solutions to simple inequalities on a number line.
  • Solve one‑step and two‑step inequalities.
  • Interpret inequality solutions in context (like “at least” or “no more than”).

Graphing

Graphing turns algebra into pictures. You’ll learn how to plot points, understand slope, and see how equations become lines.

Key idea: Slope is “rise over run.”

Example: A slope of 2 means “up 2 for every 1 to the right.”

  • Plot points given as (x, y) pairs.
  • Find slope from two points.
  • Graph lines from slope‑intercept form.
Open Graphing Lesson

Polynomials

Polynomials are expressions with multiple terms. You’ll practice adding, subtracting, and multiplying them, and start to see patterns in their structure.

Key idea: Each term is a coefficient times a variable to a power.

Example: In 3x² − 5x + 2, the terms are 3x², −5x, and 2.

  • Add and subtract polynomials by combining like terms.
  • Multiply binomials using FOIL or distribution.
  • Recognize common polynomial patterns (like (x + a)²).
Open Polynomials Lesson

Factoring

Factoring is the reverse of multiplying. You’ll learn how to pull expressions apart into simpler pieces that multiply together.

Key idea: Factoring often reveals roots and structure.

Example: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

  • Factor out the greatest common factor (GCF).
  • Factor simple trinomials.
  • Use factoring to solve equations.
Open Factoring Lesson

Quadratics

Quadratics introduce parabolas, roots, and the quadratic formula. You’ll see how algebra and graphs connect more deeply.

Key idea: A quadratic can have 0, 1, or 2 real roots.

Example: x² − 5x + 6 = 0 has roots 2 and 3.

  • Identify a, b, and c in standard form.
  • Find the vertex and axis of symmetry.
  • Use the quadratic formula to find roots.
Open Quadratics Lesson

Functions

Functions formalize the idea of input and output. You’ll learn notation, domain, range, and how to evaluate functions.

Key idea: f(x) is just a name for an output.

Example: If f(x) = 2x + 1, then f(3) = 7.

  • Evaluate functions for specific inputs.
  • Describe domain and range in words and symbols.
  • Interpret function rules in real‑world contexts.
Open Functions Lesson

Word Problems

Word problems are where algebra meets reality. You’ll practice turning stories into equations and interpreting your solutions in context.

Key idea: The hardest part is often the translation, not the solving.

Example: “Twice a number plus 3 is 11” becomes 2x + 3 = 11.

  • Identify the unknown and define variables clearly.
  • Write equations that match the situation.
  • Check whether your answer makes sense in context.
